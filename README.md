# Embodied Glucose Feedback

## Research question
**Does the way we perceive physiological data become part of the physiology it describes?**

**Can different representations of identical CGM information produce measurably different neural and autonomic responses?**

**Primary measures:** EEG + EDA + HRV

## Core framing

Health metrics are increasingly presented continuously through wearables and digital interfaces. These measurements are often treated as neutral information, yet the **way physiological data is represented may change how it is perceived, interpreted, and acted upon — and may also influence neural and autonomic state**.

Continuous glucose monitoring provides an especially useful model system because it transforms an otherwise largely invisible physiological process into continuous, personally meaningful feedback.

CGM is therefore the **primary experimental case**, while the broader scientific question is:

> **How does the representation of health data influence the psychology and physiology of the person receiving it?**

## Broader concept

The project examines whether health data should be understood not only as a measurement output, but as part of a feedback loop:

**physiology → sensing → data representation → perception → neural/autonomic response → behaviour → physiology**

A numerical value, graph, colour-coded warning, abstract visualisation or haptic cue may communicate the same underlying state, but may not produce the same experience.

The project therefore investigates whether the **form of health information itself becomes physiologically relevant once it enters perception**.

From the perspective of **predictive processing and interoception**, externally presented physiological information may become incorporated into the brain’s model of the body rather than functioning as neutral information.

We hypothesize that **less judgmental and less numerically explicit representations of glucose may preserve useful awareness while reducing unnecessary neural and autonomic reactivity to glucose fluctuations.**

## Why CGM

CGM provides a particularly strong experimental platform because:

- glucose is continuously measured;
- the data has clear personal and physiological meaning;
- the same state can be represented in many different ways;
- users often react behaviourally to values, trends and alerts;
- glucose data naturally connects perception, anticipation, decision-making and bodily regulation.

This makes CGM a focused and experimentally tractable case through which to study a much broader phenomenon.

## Novelty
Most CGM research focuses on accuracy, usability, or clinical outcomes. This project instead treats the **representation of physiological information itself as an experimental variable**.

It asks whether identical metabolic information presented numerically, graphically, abstractly, or haptically produces different physiological responses.

## Methods
Participants using CGM would receive standardized representations of the same glucose information, for example:

- numerical value and trend arrow
- conventional glucose graph
- semantic warning / high-salience display
- abstract visual feedback
- haptic feedback
- no-feedback control

Acute responses would be measured using **EEG**, **EDA**, **HRV**, and potentially pupil dilation or respiration, alongside CGM trajectories and behavioral measures such as perceived urgency, checking behavior, and correction intent.

**fMRI** could be used in an extended study to localize representation-dependent responses in brain regions involved in salience, interoception, autonomic regulation, and decision-making, while EEG provides a more practical measure of rapid stimulus-locked neural responses for the initial study.

## Feasibility
The project can begin with a compact experimental study using existing CGM systems, wearable physiological sensors, and relatively simple custom feedback interfaces.

An initial healthy-participant phase can isolate the effect of representation itself under controlled conditions. A later study in people with diabetes can test how clinically meaningful, learned glucose information changes those responses.

The initial aim is not to demonstrate long-term glycemic improvement, but to establish whether **representation alone produces measurable differences in neural and autonomic response**.

## Potential impact
The work could establish a new way of thinking about health interfaces: not simply as displays of physiology, but as components within a **closed human physiological feedback loop**.

The longer-term objective is to understand how **health-data interfaces shape the experience of the body they describe**, and whether better-designed representations can preserve useful awareness while reducing unnecessary cognitive or physiological reactivity.

Beyond diabetes, the concept could extend to:

- heart rate and HRV
- blood pressure
- sleep scores
- stress metrics
- SpO₂
- pain
- recovery/readiness scores

## Catch phrases

> Health data is not neutral once it enters perception. Its form can shape its meaning, and its meaning may shape physiology.

> When we make the invisible body visible, how does that change the body we are observing?
