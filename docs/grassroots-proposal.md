## Project topic

Health metrics are increasingly presented continuously through wearables and digital interfaces. These measurements are often treated as neutral information, yet the **way physiological data is represented may change how it is perceived, interpreted, and acted upon — and may also influence neural and autonomic state**.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides an especially useful model system because it transforms an otherwise largely invisible physiological process into continuous, personally meaningful feedback.

The central research question is:

> **Can different representations of identical CGM information produce measurably different neural and autonomic responses?**

CGM is therefore the **primary experimental case**, while the broader scientific question is:

> **How does the representation of health data influence the psychology and physiology of the person receiving it?**

The project examines whether health data should be understood not only as a measurement output, but as part of a feedback loop:

**physiology → sensing → data representation → perception → neural/autonomic response → behaviour → physiology**

A numerical value, graph, colour-coded warning, abstract visualisation or haptic cue may communicate the same underlying state, but may not produce the same experience.

The project therefore investigates whether the **form of health information itself becomes physiologically relevant once it enters perception**.

CGM is particularly well suited to this because glucose is continuously measured, personally meaningful, behaviourally actionable, and easily represented in multiple formats. This makes it a focused experimental model through which to study a broader phenomenon relevant to digital health and wearable technologies.

## Objectives

1. Determine whether different representations of identical glucose information produce different **neural and autonomic responses**.
2. Compare conventional numerical/graphical CGM feedback with **abstract, low-salience and haptic representations**.
3. Relate representation-dependent responses to **perceived urgency, attention and intended behaviour**.
4. Establish a reusable experimental platform for later studies involving people with diabetes and real-world CGM interaction.

**Primary measures:** EEG + EDA + HRV  
**Extended methods:** pupil/respiration measures and, where feasible, fMRI for spatial localisation of representation-dependent neural responses.

---

## Scientific context and gap

Personalised glucose feedback has already been shown to evoke measurable brain activity during fMRI, demonstrating that physiological feedback can act as a meaningful neural stimulus (Whelan et al., 2017). Interoceptive-inference models further suggest that perception of bodily state participates in predictive and autonomic regulation (Owens et al., 2018).

CGM research also shows that glucose information can provide reassurance and control while also producing overload, distress and alert burden (Natale et al., 2023). Recent work shows that device alerts can drive concrete behaviours such as glucose overcorrection (Everett et al., 2026).

However, relatively little work isolates **how the format of otherwise identical physiological information changes neural and autonomic processing**.

The proposed study therefore focuses on:

**same physiological information → different sensory representation → different brain/autonomic response?**

## Feasibility and study concept

### Phase 1 — Controlled study in healthy participants

Healthy participants provide a practical first population in which to isolate the effects of representation without major confounds from insulin dosing, previous severe hypoglycaemia, treatment habits or diabetes-related fear.

Participants would be exposed to standardized glucose states presented in several formats, for example:

- exact numerical value + trend arrow
- conventional CGM graph
- semantic warning / high-salience display
- abstract visual representation
- haptic representation
- low-information or neutral control

The underlying glucose information remains identical across conditions.

Acute responses would be measured using **EEG, EDA and HRV**, with behavioural/subjective measures such as perceived urgency, salience, comprehension and intended action.

### Phase 2 — Translation to diabetes

A later study would repeat the paradigm in people with diabetes, where glucose information carries learned, clinically meaningful and interoceptive significance.

This enables comparison between:

- response to representation itself;
- learned meaning of glucose information;
- lived experience of glucose management.

## Potential impact

The project could establish a new way of evaluating health interfaces: not only by asking whether information is accurate or understandable, but whether **its representation alters the physiological and behavioural state of the person receiving it**.

The findings could inform the design of future CGM interfaces that communicate necessary information while controlling unnecessary salience, cognitive load or autonomic reactivity.

More broadly, the project positions CGM as a model system for a general question in digital health:

> **How do health-data interfaces shape the experience of the body they describe?**

The same principles could extend to:

- heart rate / HRV
- blood pressure
- sleep metrics
- stress scores
- SpO₂
- pain
- recovery/readiness scores

The longer-term objective is to understand whether better-designed health-data representations can preserve useful awareness while reducing unnecessary cognitive or physiological reactivity.

> **Health data is not neutral once it enters perception. Its form can shape its meaning, and its meaning may shape physiology.**

## Relevance to the Grassroots program

The project is well suited to a Grassroots format because it depends on the combination of capabilities that are rarely contained within a single discipline:

- **design and prototyping** to create controlled sensory representations;
- **neuroscience** to measure how those representations are processed;
- **physiology** to quantify autonomic response;
- **human-computer interaction** to understand perception and behaviour;
- **clinical expertise** for later translation into diabetes.

The study is deliberately scoped as an exploratory, technically feasible pilot that can generate both **research data and a reusable experimental platform**. Its value comes from the synergy between disciplines rather than from any single technology.
